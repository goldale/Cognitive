Project Goal

Today's large language models are already capable of understanding language, reasoning, planning, and generating coherent responses. Their greatest limitation is not their ability to think, but their inability to continuously accumulate, organize, and reuse knowledge throughout the lifetime of the system.

The goal of the Cognitive Architecture is to overcome this limitation while preserving the strengths of the Transformer. Rather than replacing the Transformer with an entirely new architecture, the Cognitive Architecture starts with an existing, well-established Transformer and extends it with one additional capability: an external Associative Memory.

The modification itself is surprisingly small.

In the canonical implementation, one internal layer located approximately in the middle of the Transformer is expanded by a factor of two.

The original neurons continue to perform exactly the same computation as before.

At the same time, their internal activation state is made available to the external Associative Memory.

The additional neurons create a direct neural interface between the Transformer and an external Associative Memory.

One half of this interface carries information from the Transformer to the Associative Memory, allowing the memory to learn directly from the Transformer's own internal language.

The other half carries information from the Associative Memory back to the Transformer, allowing relevant long-term knowledge to return encoded in exactly the same internal language.

From the Transformer's perspective, interaction with long-term memory becomes part of its normal forward computation. No separate reasoning mechanism or external retrieval process is required.

The result is a clear separation of responsibilities.

The Transformer continues to think. It understands language, performs reasoning, constructs plans, and generates responses.

The Associative Memory remembers. It preserves the system's Adaptive Long-Term State, accumulates experience, stores learned knowledge, and continuously develops new associations over time.

Neither subsystem attempts to perform the role of the other.

Instead, they operate together as a single cognitive system by communicating through a Shared Internal Language that naturally exists inside the Transformer itself.

This architectural separation is the foundation of the Cognitive Architecture. Thinking and long-term adaptation become independent responsibilities while remaining tightly integrated through a common neural language. Every component described in the remainder of this document follows from this single architectural principle.
